### Three Things to Know About Identity Theft December is a busy time. When a letter arrived saying I had been denied a credit card, I didn't think much of it. I hadn't applied for a card, so I assumed it was a mistake. I went back to my wassail and wondered whether Dasher or Donner got to lead

the sleigh on clear nights. Then the bills started coming. One company, then another, then another. Someone had stolen my identity and opened accounts in my name. They ran up more than \$10,000 in charges. Like many people, I had assumed identity theft meant someone got access to my existing credit cards. I hadn't considered that thieves might open brand new accounts using my name, Social Security number, birth date, and address. But they had all of it. What followed was an agonizing process of trying to reclaim my identity. If you haven't been through this, I'm sorry to say you might not be immune. Our personal data has been exposed many times over the years, and the internet has no shortage of it. You can check how much of your information is floating around online, and you may be surprised at what's out there. If identity theft ever happens to you, here are three steps that helped me get back on track.
### 1. Contact Creditors Directly If you receive bills for accounts you didn't open, call

the creditor. You aren't liable for charges you didn't authorize. Ask to be connected to their fraud department. Most creditors will investigate, confirm it wasn't you, and remove the accounts from your record and credit report.
### 2. Check All Three Credit Reports Review your credit reports from Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion. They don't always contain

the same information, so check each carefully. Dispute anything suspicious. In my case, the fraudsters inverted my first and middle names, so a name I'd never used showed up on my report. I was able to have it removed. You may also spot creditors you haven't heard from yet. I discovered another \$5,000 in fraudulent charges this way.
### 3. File a Police Report and FTC Affidavit Go to your local police station with all your documentation. File a report and ask for a copy --- you'll need it

for the credit bureaus. Then, go to [identitytheft.gov](https://www.identitytheft.gov/) and complete the FTC affidavit. Submit that to the credit bureaus as well. Having my identity stolen was unsettling. I thought I had been careful. In today's world, though, it's almost impossible to keep private information truly private. And while the idea of going off the grid might sound romantic, it isn't practical if you want to live and work in a city.
